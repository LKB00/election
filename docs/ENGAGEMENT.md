# Build a daily ritual, not a game

The research points one way. Election gets more votes, more return visits and more shares by doing three things. It sharpens the moments people already enjoy: the guess, the reveal, and "you vs everyone". It turns today's question into a **short daily set with a clear end**, like Wordle. And it makes every result easy to forward on WhatsApp **without giving away the vote**. It does not need points, streaks or prizes. Strong research says those can kill the fun they are meant to boost. Across 128 experiments, rewards tied to doing or finishing a task reduced people's later free-choice interest (d = −0.40 and −0.36), and the effect was stronger in children ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf)). The products in this space that lasted, like Wordle and NYT Games, give a small daily dose that is the same for everyone and an honest way to share it. The ones that blew up and then died (HQ Trivia, Gas, tbh, NGL, Sarahah) ran on prizes, anxiety, fake activity or anonymous nastiness. The top new features below, in order, are: a finite "Today's set" with an end card, a spoiler-safe text share for WhatsApp, sharper result lines ("rare take"), a real evening "final count" for today's question, and a fast "make a poll for your group" flow with a "your poll has 37 votes" return. The one number to steer by is **weekly returning voters**: people who voted on two or more different days in the last 7. You can count it today from the voter cookie the site already uses for one vote per person, with no login. A caution: most of the evidence comes from lab studies and from other products. None of it is a test of a poll site in India. So treat the impact ratings as informed guesses, and test the big ones.

## Five human drives explain why people vote, come back and share

**The gap between "what I think" and "what everyone thinks" is the engine.** Psychologists call this the information gap. People feel a small itch when they notice something they don't know but want to know ([Loewenstein 1994](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75)). The itch is strongest when people are *unsure* of the answer. In brain-scan and trivia studies, curiosity peaked at middle confidence. Answers people were curious about were still remembered two weeks later, and curiosity switched on the brain's reward system ([Kang et al. 2009](https://neuroecon.berkeley.edu/public/papers/Psychol%20Sci%202009%20Kang.pdf)). Making a guess first raises curiosity further ([Brod & Breitwieser 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-019-0056-y)). That is why "Guess the crowd" before the reveal is your strongest move. One surprising finding matters for decisions. In six experiments, people enjoyed "first a mystery, then the answer" more than getting everything at once. Yet when asked, they *said* they would prefer everything at once ([Ruan, Hsee & Lu 2018, summarised by CBS](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/107498331/Psychology_and_Marketing_-_2024_-_Mandler_-_This_article_is_Consumer_reactions_to_unfinished_teasers_for_digital_content.pdf)). So if users ever ask you to "just show the results", don't remove the seal because of that request alone. What people say they want and what they enjoy can differ.

**"Am I normal?" is the second engine, and surprise is what gets shared.** People have a basic drive to check their opinions against others ([Festinger 1954](https://doi.org/10.1177/001872675400700202)). Talking about yourself is rewarding in itself. In experiments, people gave up money to answer questions about themselves ([Tamir & Mitchell 2012](https://www.pnas.org/doi/abs/10.1073/pnas.1202129109)). People also assume their own view is the common one. In the classic study, students who agreed to a task guessed about 62% of others would agree too, and students who refused guessed only about 33% ([False consensus effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_consensus_effect)). For Election, this means many voters expect to be in the majority. So "only 1 in 8 picked this" is a real surprise. Surprise is energising, and energising feelings like awe and amusement predict sharing ([Berger & Milkman 2012](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmr.10.0353)). Personal "you vs a big crowd" results are proven crowd-pullers. The NYT's dialect quiz gave each person a personal map built from 350,000+ survey answers. It was the paper's most-viewed piece of 2013, even though it came out on 21 December ([Knight Lab](https://knightlab.northwestern.edu/2014/01/20/behind-the-dialect-map-interactive-how-an-intern-created-the-new-york-times-most-popular-piece-of-content-in-2013/)). Some of that ranking came from each question counting as a page view ([CJR](https://www.cjr.org/the_kicker/on_that_most_read_nyt_intern_p.php)).

**"One more" works, but only while the person stays in control.** Each poll's result is naturally unpredictable, so the next poll is already a small surprise. You don't need to add any. The strongest data on "next item" comes from autoplay. In a randomised study of Netflix users, turning autoplay off cut viewing by **21 minutes a day** ([Schaffner et al. 2025](https://brennanschaffner.com/wp-content/uploads/Autoplay_Netflix_Experiment.pdf)). But YouTube users named autoplay as the feature that most undermined their sense of control, while search and playlists supported it ([Lukoff et al. 2021](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3411764.3445467)). Your tap-to-advance "Next poll" deck sits on the right side of that line. Keep it there. One well-known idea doesn't hold up: the "Zeigarnik effect", the claim that unfinished tasks stick in memory. A 2025 meta-analysis found unfinished and finished tasks were remembered equally (ratio about 0.99). What did hold up is a related effect: people tend to *go back to* interrupted tasks ([Ghibellini & Meier 2025](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Interruption,-recall-and-resumption:-a-of-the-and-Ghibellini-Meier/c98a41f13fd6f3b0b86047ecf92eeb03ac458626)). So a gentle "2 of today's polls left" for a returning visitor makes sense. A design that counts on people being haunted by unfinished polls does not.

**A shared daily moment brings people back. A count you can lose does not need to.** Wordle grew from **90 players on 1 Nov 2021 to over 2 million weekly players by 9 Jan 2022**, and **1.2 million results were shared on Twitter in the first 13 days of January** ([Wikipedia: Wordle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wordle)). The growth came after the coloured-square share grid was added. Players invented that grid themselves ([Wardle on X](https://x.com/powerlanguish/status/1471493886031773707?lang=en)). Its creator wanted "three minutes of your time a day, and that's it", and said the social side came from everyone solving the same puzzle ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/marketing/josh-wardle-reflects-on-the-the-unconventional-road-to-wordle-s-success)). Wordle did show a streak, but the grid drove the growth, not the streak. After the peak, players settled at about half, which was still huge (5.3 billion plays in 2024) ([Wikipedia: NYT Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times_Games)). BeReal shows the opposite. Its one daily alert built a habit, but it came to feel like a chore. Daily users roughly halved within months ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2023/02/22/bereal-may-be-on-the-out-users-have-nearly-halved-since-peak/), [Wikipedia: BeReal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BeReal)). Lesson: the daily question must be worth opening on its own. It cannot rely on guilt or obligation.

**Small "nearly done" and "I made this" effects transfer cleanly, without points.** In a car-wash field test, a 10-stamp card with 2 stamps already filled was completed by **34%** of customers. A plain 8-stamp card, which needed exactly the same 8 washes, was completed by only **19%** ([Nunes & Drèze 2006](https://doi.org/10.1086/500480), [summary](https://jonathanbecher.com/2020/05/24/endowed-progress-effect/)). People also value things they built themselves more highly. This is called the IKEA effect ([Norton, Mochon & Ariely 2012](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/11-091.pdf)). And people remember an experience mostly by its best moment and its last moment. This "peak-end" effect was first shown for pain, and it also holds for pleasant experiences ([Do, Rupert & Wolford 2008](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/PBR.15.1.96.pdf)). Put together: give people a *short set* that starts partly done, end it on a good card, and give creators a reason to check how their poll is doing. Gamification research supports your ban on game parts. Across studies, gamification gives only small average gains that depend heavily on context ([Hamari et al. 2014](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/does-gamification-work-a-literature-review-of-empirical-studies-o/), [Sailer & Homner 2020](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1245270)). In one semester-long class, badges, leaderboards and coins *lowered* motivation, satisfaction and exam scores ([Hanus & Fox 2015](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/265644737_Assessing_the_effects_of_gamification_in_the_classroom_A_longitudinal_study_on_intrinsic_motivation_social_comparison_satisfaction_effort_and_academic_performance)).

## India adds four facts the design must respect

**Every forward has to earn itself.** India forwards more on WhatsApp than any other country. After rumour-driven mob killings in 2018, WhatsApp capped forwards at 5 chats in India ([The Hill](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/398121-whatsapp-to-limit-message-forwarding-following-murders-spurred-by/)). Since 2020, a "frequently forwarded" message can go to only one chat at a time. That cut highly-forwarded messages by **70%** ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/04/27/whatsapps-new-limit-cuts-virality-of-highly-forwarded-messages-by-70)). So chain messages die fast. What travels is a *personal* result that each person chooses to re-send. WhatsApp's Status and Channels area (the "Updates" tab) is used daily by about **1.5 billion people worldwide** ([Meta](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/06/helping-you-find-more-channels-businesses-on-whatsapp/)). That makes a tall Status card and a daily Channel post natural fits. Trust is also fragile. **53%** of Indian respondents named WhatsApp as the biggest misinformation threat, the highest of any country surveyed ([Reuters Institute DNR 2025](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/india)). So every card should say plainly "fun poll, not a survey".

**The day has two natural moments: waking up and late evening.** **84%** of Indian smartphone users check their phone within 15 minutes of waking ([BOOM, citing BCG](https://www.boomlive.in/news/84-indians-check-phones-within-15-minutes-of-waking-up-report-24356)). Phone use is reported to peak between 8pm and midnight. That source is weaker and its method is unclear ([Analytics Insight](https://www.analyticsinsight.net/phones/india-the-mobile-nation-how-the-smartphone-has-reinvented-the-nations-digital-life)). The "good morning" image habit is so strong that in 2018 Google found about **1 in 3** Indian smartphones ran out of storage because of greeting images ([YourStory](https://yourstory.com/2018/01/google-whatsapp-good-morning)). A morning question that is fun to post in a family group, followed by an evening "how everyone voted" moment, fits this rhythm.

**Language and phones decide who can join.** India has about **886 million** active internet users, more of them rural than urban, and **57% prefer content in their own language**. About **1 in 5 use someone else's phone** ([BusinessToday on IAMAI–Kantar 2024](https://www.businesstoday.in/technology/news/story/indias-internet-revolution-key-insights-from-kantar-and-iamai-report-461043-2025-01-16)). Shared phones mean one cookie can cover a whole family. So never build "your voting history" screens that could show one person's votes to another person on the same phone. Speed still matters, even though data is cheap. Google's older data shows **53%** of mobile visitors leave a page that takes more than 3 seconds ([Think with Google](https://business.google.com/ca-en/think/marketing-strategies/mobile-page-speed-new-industry-benchmarks/)). In India, Flipkart's lightweight web app got **60% of visits from its home-screen icon** and **40% higher re-engagement** ([web.dev](https://web.dev/case-studies/flipkart)). Both results are from 2016–18, so they are old but still the best available.

**Indians already vote on two-tap polls when results show instantly.** Inshorts' "Youth of the Nation" poll drew more than 150,000 users and about 16 lakh answers ([Inshorts](https://inshorts.com/en/news/inshorts-releases-findings-of-youth-of-the-nation-poll-1472988564048)). Its ad polls show results right after the tap ([The Media Ant](https://www.themediaant.com/digital/inshorts-advertising)). Cricket is the biggest moment everyone shares. The 2025 IPL final hit **55 million people watching at once** on JioHotstar ([JioStar](https://www.jiostar.com/news/from-stadiums-to-screens-jiostars-tata-ipl-2025-a-year-of-firsts-report-highlights-how-a-billion-viewers-came-together-to-celebrate-cricket/)). IPL teams are city identities (Chennai, Mumbai, Bengaluru). That gives you a ready-made, friendly "city vs city" angle with no location tracking.

**Why Today's set comes first.** It combines four effects with good evidence and needs no rule-breaking parts. It gives a reason to come tomorrow, as Wordle's one-a-day did. It gives a head start: arriving from a shared link counts as "1 of 5 done", which is the car-wash card's two free stamps ([Nunes & Drèze 2006](https://doi.org/10.1086/500480)). It ends on a high point, the "your day vs everyone" card. And it gives a clear stopping point, which research links to time people don't regret spending. In a CHI 2025 study, people often regretted endless scrolling they did to cope with bad moods or to put off work ([Scrolling in the Deep](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3706598.3713187)). The end card is also a ready share image. One design warning: your rules ban patricka's steppers. So show progress as a short text line ("2 left today"), not as dots or a step bar. And the set must reset each day, with no count kept across days, or it quietly becomes a streak. After the end card, the existing Next-poll deck can stay available for people who want more. Don't push it.

**Why the text share matters as much as the image.** Wordle's grid spread because it showed *how you did* without spoiling *the answer*, and it pasted anywhere as plain text ([Wikipedia: Wordle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wordle)). Your version shows which side of the split you landed on, not your choice, so the secret ballot stays intact. The receiver has to vote to find out. That is the same curiosity gap that makes the reveal work. A text line is lighter than an image on WhatsApp, and it can carry a share tag in the link so you can measure it (see the next section). Keep the existing story card with QR for Status. Add the text line as the quick, low-data option.

**Why the result lines need a test.** Theory says "rare take" lines should be the most shared, because they surprise people who expected to be in the majority. Theory also says "you're with the 63%" gives comfort. No study compares these wordings ([psychology notes; Berger & Milkman 2012](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmr.10.0353)). Pick whichever line is true for that voter, then A/B test the wording against share-tap rate. Show guess accuracy plainly for one poll at a time ("You guessed 60%, it was 64%"). Don't add it up across polls, and avoid the word "points". A running total is a score, and scores are banned.

**Why an evening final count, and why it must be real.** Real deadlines are fine. Fake ones are "false urgency", which India's 2023 dark-pattern guidelines ban ([IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/india-s-ccpa-guidelines-on-dark-patterns-welcome-signal-but-law-is-still-soft)). A crawl of about 11,000 shopping sites found dozens of fake countdown timers ([Mathur et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.07032)). A real 9pm close for today's question gives morning voters a reason to come back in the evening peak, and gives group admins a moment to post "here's how we voted". The calendar reminder you already have fits event polls (an IPL final). It fits a daily habit less well. The Channel and the evening reveal handle the daily part. If today's question touches politics, Election mode and the silence-window rules still apply.

**Why creators and group admins are worth special care.** Repeat use on Slido and StrawPoll comes from hosts who bring a new audience each time, not from voters' habits. Slido reports 55M+ participants, all joining without accounts ([Poll Everywhere comparison](https://www.polleverywhere.com/use-cases/vs/poll-everywhere-vs-slido)). The "share your personal link on Stories" loop gave Sarahah about 300 million users in 2017 ([Wikipedia: Sarahah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarahah)). Election can use that loop safely because answers are choices, not free-text messages about a person. tbh reached 2.5 million daily users in nine weeks with ready-made, positive questions, so nobody faced a blank page ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2017/10/16/facebook-acquires-anonymous-teen-compliment-app-tbh-will-let-it-run/)). Templates do the same job. Remember "my polls" only on the phone, and show the live vote count when the creator returns. That is the IKEA effect at work.

**Two ideas that are not on the list.** Auto-advancing to the next poll would add time on site, but it is exactly the autoplay pattern that research ties to lost control. The EU's 2025 rules for platforms used by minors say autoplay, streaks and infinite scroll should be off by default for them ([Hogan Lovells](https://www.hlc.com/en/publications/the-longawaited-eu-guidelines-on-article-281-dsa-what-online-platforms-must-know)). Web push notifications are also left out. They are weak on iPhone without a home-screen install, they need opt-in, and night-time pushes are a listed concern for minors. The Channel does the same job more politely.

## Count returning voters, not page views

**North Star: weekly returning voters.** This is the number of distinct voter IDs that voted on **2 or more different days in the last 7**. It rewards a habit people value, not time spent. Duolingo shows why a "keep current users" metric is the right lever. When it focused on its current-user retention rate, daily churn among current users fell more than 40%, and daily users grew about 4.5x over four years ([How They Grow](https://www.howtheygrow.co/p/how-duolingo-grows)). Much of Duolingo's lift came from streaks and leaderboards, which you don't use. So copy the metric, not the tactics. Avoid "engagement" scores that reward reactions and comments. Facebook's "Meaningful Social Interactions" metric was shown to boost outrage and divisive political content ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2021/10/27/tech/facebook-papers-meaningful-social-interaction-news-feed-math)), and politics polls are where that risk lives for you.

**Activation (the "it clicked" moment).** A first-time visitor counts as activated when they vote, see the reveal, and then **vote on a second poll or make a guess in the same visit**. Track the share of new visitors who reach this point. Then check whether it predicts coming back within 7 days, and adjust the threshold (one extra vote? two?) to the version that best separates people who return from people who don't.

**Benchmarks to aim for.** These are rough, because the figures come from vendor reports about apps, not mobile websites. A typical cross-industry app keeps about 25% of new users on day 1, about 12% on day 7 and about 6% on day 30. Social apps keep about 40%, 18% and 12% ([UXCam 2026](https://uxcam.com/blog/mobile-app-retention-benchmarks/), [Apsteq 2026](https://apsteq.com/blog/app-retention-benchmarks/)). A website has no app icon and no push, so **day 1 at 20–30%, day 7 at 8–12% and day 30 at 4–6% would be acceptable**. Reaching social-app levels would be excellent. For "stickiness" (daily users divided by monthly users), 20%+ is strong for general apps, and social apps often pass 50% ([vmobify, secondary](https://vmobify.com/blog/dau-mau-stickiness-benchmarks)). Track **votes per visit** (median) and **share taps per voter** too.

**Sharing: measure how far one share travels.** This is called the K-factor: shares per voter times the share of those shares that bring in a new voter. Speed matters as much as size. Above 1, with a 3-day cycle, the audience doubles in about 18 days; with a 30-day cycle it takes about 6 months ([Ideaplan](https://www.ideaplan.io/metrics/viral-coefficient-k-factor)). To measure it without login, add a short tag to every shared link (`?s=` plus a random sharer code). Count the share-button taps. Then count new voter IDs whose first visit carries a tag. Forwards often strip tags, so the number you get is a floor, not the real total.

**How to measure all this without login, and keep it DPDP-safe.** The signed voter cookie that already enforces one vote per person can feed simple server-side tables: first day seen against the days active. That gives day-1, day-7 and day-30 return rates with no outside tool. Cookieless tools like Plausible can't do this, because they reset visitor IDs every day by design ([Plausible](https://plausible.io/cookieless-web-analytics)). Keep this strictly as **group totals**. Section 9(3) of India's DPDP Act bans tracking, behavioural monitoring and targeted ads aimed at under-18s, even with parent consent. Its main duties start 13 May 2027 ([dpdpindia.in](https://www.dpdpindia.in/dpdp-children.html), secondary summary). A no-login site can't check ages. So: no per-person profiles, no personalised ranking built from someone's history, no ad targeting, and delete raw logs on a schedule. "Remember my topics" can live on the phone only. Remember that shared phones and cleared cookies make all of these numbers rough.

**Watch the safety numbers next to the growth numbers.** Track the share of visits that reach the end card (healthy stopping). Ask an occasional one-tap question after a set ("Good use of your time? Yes / Meh / No"). Also track the report rate, and the share of traffic going to politics polls, which warns you early if things drift toward outrage.

**Test only big changes, and for long enough.** A small site can only detect large effects. A common rule of thumb gives the visitors needed per test group as about 16 × p × (1 − p) ÷ δ², where p is the starting rate and δ is the change you want to detect ([Evan Miller](https://www.evanmiller.org/ab-testing/sample-size.html)). Example: if 10% of voters tap share and you want to detect a rise to 12%, you need about **3,600 visitors per group**. At 500 new visitors a day, that is about two weeks. A 5% change would need about 16 times more traffic, which isn't realistic. Split groups by voter ID, not by page view. Run tests for full weeks, and decide your main metric before you start. Don't stop the moment the numbers look good. Shiny new things get a first-week bump that fades by weeks 3–6. One "winning" Airbnb test turned out to be noise when it was left running ([LogRocket](https://blog.logrocket.com/product-management/novelty-effect-ab-testing/)). Keep 1–2% of visitors on the old version for a few weeks after you pick a winner. For share features, judge results by the sharer's group, because a share sent from version B can land on someone in version A.

## The fastest-growing poll apps died of the tricks you must avoid

The failures share a pattern. HQ Trivia peaked at **2.38 million live players** in March 2018. Cheaters and novelty fade wore it down, and it shut in February 2020 ([Wikipedia: HQ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HQ_(video_game)), [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/02/14/hq-trivia-shuts-down)). Gas reached 1 million daily users in two months. It sold paid hints about who voted for you, then shut about 15 months after launch amid "a steep decline in users" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/10/19/discord-kills-gas-anonymous-compliments-app-bought-nine-months-ago/)). NGL sent **computer-generated fake messages** made to look like they came from real friends, to push a paid upgrade. It paid **$5 million** and was banned from serving under-18s ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/07/ftc-order-will-ban-ngl-labs-its-founders-offering-anonymous-messaging-apps-kids-under-18-halt)). Sarahah was pulled from app stores over bullying ([Wikipedia: Sarahah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarahah)). India's Koo removed only 6 of 23 reported hate posts in one test and closed in 2024 ([Global Witness](https://globalwitness.org/en/press-releases/youtube-and-indian-social-media-platform-koo-enable-misogynistic-hate-speech-violating-platform-policies/)). Your rules already block prizes, paid reveals and points. The remaining risks are fake activity, polls about people, and pressure tactics.

India's dark-pattern guidelines list 13 banned tricks ([Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=f2dbef73-a214-4786-be4f-63a9c72e097e)). Several apply to a free poll site. This table shows where each of your engagement tools crosses the line.

| Tool | Healthy version | Becomes a dark pattern when |
|---|---|---|
| Sealed results | Reveal right after voting | "Share to see results" (forced action) |
| Next poll | Tap to continue; clear end card | Auto-advance, or a bottomless feed with no end |
| Today's question deadline | Real closing time | Fake countdown or "closing soon" that never closes (false urgency) |
| Vote counts | Exactly the database number | Inflated counts, seeded votes, "someone just voted" made up (NGL) |
| Friend prompt | "Your friend voted. See if you match" (only when true) | "Don't let Priya down" (confirm-shaming) |
| Home-screen / Channel ask | Asked once, at a natural moment | Asked again after "Not now" (nagging) |
| Share vs Done buttons | Share is the main button, Done is clearly visible | Exit hidden or greyed out to block leaving (interface interference) |
| Guess accuracy | "You guessed 60%, it was 64%", for this poll only | Added up into a running score or ranking |
| You vs everyone | Honest split, friendly words | Mocking people in the minority |

Four more things to avoid. **Never claim results show what India thinks.** Self-selected online polls are badly skewed compared with real voters ([arXiv 2406.03340](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.03340v1)). So label results "people who voted here". This matters even more for politics, and the words "exit poll" must never appear. **Don't allow polls that pit religions, castes or communities against each other, or polls about private individuals.** These get screenshotted out of context on a platform where rumours have led to real violence. **Don't build screens that show someone's personal voting history**, because one phone often serves a whole family. **Don't trust early bumps** from confetti, "hot" labels or "new" tags until a 3–6 week holdout confirms them.

## Conclusion

The surprising finding is how little Election needs to *add*. Its core loop is already the one the psychology recommends: guess, vote, reveal, compare. The biggest gains come from **shaping time**, not from new mechanics. That means a small set that starts partly done and ends cleanly, a morning question with a real evening close, and a share that teases without spoiling. These turn a good single visit into a daily habit without a streak. The rules, which may look like handicaps against Duolingo-style growth, are actually protection. Every fast-rising, fast-dying product in this space broke one of them. Several of the same features (finite sets, no autoplay, no profiling) are what EU and Indian regulators now expect for sites minors can reach.

Three unknowns deserve your own small experiments rather than more reading. Which result line ("rare take" or "with the majority") gets forwarded more in India? Does Hinglish or English share better? And does a WhatsApp Channel actually bring people back, given that nobody has published click data for Channels? Weekly returning voters, a share tag on every link, and a two-week A/B test are enough to answer all three.
